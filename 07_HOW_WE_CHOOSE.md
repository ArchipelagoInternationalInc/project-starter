# 07 — HOW WE CHOOSE
*Archipelago Studio Kit v3. Drafted September 2026, ratified October 2026, changed by Scott November 5, 2026 (see "Changing this file" at the end). This file is about seeing a product's market clearly before and while we build it. It gives perspective. It never decides for Scott.*

---

## What this file is for

Before the studio spends weeks on a product, it helps to know who already serves the same people, what they charge, and how buyers would find us. A day of research can save months of surprise. That is the whole point of this file.

**This file never blocks a build.** Nothing here is pass or fail. No check, score or brief can veto a product, park it, or stop work on it. Many products that did very well in the world would have looked weak on paper at the start. Scott decides what gets built; the research is there so he decides with his eyes open.

## The reality check

Three questions, answered with a quick search, not a guess. They take about fifteen minutes and go at the top of every Market Brief.

1. **Who pays?** The person who would actually hand over a credit card. Sometimes it's the person who uses the product; sometimes it's someone else, such as an employer, an agency or a teacher. Name them.
2. **Why us instead of the free option?** Most things have a free or cheap version somewhere: inside software people already pay for, a free website, a $5 app, a YouTube video. Name the best free option and say, in one or two sentences, what we would do that it doesn't.
3. **What's the cheapest way to find out?** The smallest, cheapest thing that would show whether real people want it: a landing page, a handful of conversations, a small ad test, a free trial. Name it and roughly what it would cost.

The answers are information. An honest "the free option is pretty good" is useful to know, and it often points to the angle worth building. It is not a reason the product can't go ahead.

## The Market Brief

One document, the same shape every time, so briefs can be compared with each other. It is built by a sourced research task, so it is checked rather than remembered. Usually it runs in the project's own chat, which holds the history; it can run anywhere that has the project's records.

It opens with the reality check above, then five sections:

1. **The customer and the job.** Who, in what moment, and who actually does the task.
2. **Who already serves them.** Three lists with prices: the big funded companies; the small companies that do only this; and the free options.
3. **How they buy.** How they pay, what they already pay for similar things, and any seasonal or timing pattern.
4. **How they would hear of us.** The specific places these customers actually spend time; the message that would move them; the timing; and a rough cost to win one paying customer compared with what they would pay. A budget to get noticed is a fair line here.
5. **What it adds up to, with numbers.** An honest rough revenue range with a stated confidence; the single biggest risk; and the cheapest four-week test that would teach us the most.

Every claim in a brief is labeled: sourced (read on a page that's linked), estimated (worked out from sourced numbers), or inferred (judgment). Inferred claims are welcome; they just have to be labeled.

## The small test (optional, often worth it)

A landing page and a small spend — a few hundred dollars — in the places the brief named, to see whether real strangers click, sign up, or say they would pay. It's cheap, it's fast, and Scott's advertising background makes it a strength. If a test is run, write down beforehand what result would count as encouraging, so the result is easy to read afterwards. A test spends money, so it runs only on Scott's yes. A disappointing result is information for Scott, not a veto.

## Who reads it

- **The project chat** (or whichever seat has the project's records) gathers the evidence and writes the brief.
- **The Studio Head** reads it and gives Scott a short, plain read on what it suggests, including the strongest reasons for and against. The seat that gathered the evidence doesn't write the final read on its own work — the same idea as builder and critic.
- **Scott decides.** He can build, wait, change direction, or ignore the brief entirely.
- **The kit** holds only the format, so every brief looks like every other.

## The launch plan

Before a product goes public, it gets a **launch plan**: channels, message, timing, budget, and what result would count as encouraging — the same shape as brief section 4, written fresh, because things change between a brief and a launch. This is planning help, not a gate.

## What already exists

Products that already have real users or signed partners — CheckThenFix, ShallFinder, and the SendMyLicense family (the engine, Steel Ticket, and the nurses product) — don't need a Market Brief, though they still get a launch plan. Any other product can have a brief whenever it would help Scott see it more clearly; it is never a precondition for work. A product that is set aside keeps its brief attached, so the reason is a document, not a memory. A set-aside product with a finished engine is not dead: the engine is inventory, reusable inside the next product that has a buyer. Decisions Scott has already made about specific products stand until he reopens them.

## What this replaces

The July 2026 selection formula (large organizations, small definable problem, website sells it, no person-to-person contact) is folded into the reality check and the brief as things worth looking at, not as rules.

## Changing this file

As with every kit file: by Scott, on purpose, recorded here. No rule changes by exception, workaround, or forgetting.

- **November 5, 2026 — changed by Scott.** The "fifteen-minute kill checks", the pass/fail rubric with hard thresholds, and the rule that no product could be worked on without a ruled Market Brief are retired. Scott's reason, in his words: "almost nothing would ever get built if I actually use this as a determinator. There are many successful projects out in the world that have done very well and made a lot of money that would not have passed this kill check system." They are replaced by the three-question reality check (who pays, why us instead of the free option, the cheapest way to find out). The Market Brief stays as perspective. Nothing in this file can block a build.

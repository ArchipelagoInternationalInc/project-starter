# 06 — HOW WE CHOOSE
*Archipelago Studio Kit v2, added September 2026. This file governs which products get built. It exists because two finished products were set aside in one day for reasons a day of research would have found before the first line of code. Choosing is the studio's real product; building is the cheap part.*

---

## The law of this file

**No product gets a build packet, a domain, a name, or a line of code until it has a ruled Market Brief.** A build packet written without one is not inventory; it is a mistake. The old kit answered the engineering question first ("does the engine work?"). That was the wrong first question. The first question is: *who already does this for free, and how would anyone find out we exist?*

## Why the analysis comes first

A build costs two to three months of studio attention. A Market Brief costs one day and a research run. Three good builds could replace the owner's day job; three bad ones cost most of a year. The decision is therefore worth more than the build, and it gets more rigor, not less.

## The five stages

Each stage is cheaper than the next one is expensive. Each has a written kill rule. An idea that dies at stage two cost a coffee; that is the process succeeding.

### Stage 1 — Intake
One paragraph, in plain words: who has the problem, what the job is, who would pay, and who actually does the task today. Unlimited. Costs nothing. Lives on the shelf.

### Stage 2 — The fifteen-minute kill checks
Four questions, answered with a search, not a guess. Any "yes" to the first two or "no" to the last two ends the idea here.

1. **Does the customer already get this free inside something they already pay for?** Their bank, their accounting software, their employer's portal, their insurer's website, YouTube. (This killed the W-9 tool: QuickBooks does it free.)
2. **Is there a small company whose entire existence is this one thing?** If so, they have a head start on every search term and every customer. (This also killed the W-9 tool: GetW9.)
3. **Does the customer do the task themselves?** Or does an intermediary — an agency, an employer, an association — do it for them? If someone else does it, they are the customer, and they may already have a solution. (This killed the practitioner version of SendMyLicense: the staffing agency sends the documents, not the nurse.)
4. **Can they buy without a salesperson?** A credit card on a website, an app store, a recommendation from an association. If the sale needs a phone call, we cannot make it.

### Stage 3 — The Market Brief (Gate 0)
One document, the same shape every time, built by a sourced research task so it is verified rather than remembered. Run in the project's own chat, which holds the context. Five sections:

1. **The customer and the job.** Who, in what moment, and who actually does the task.
2. **Who already serves them.** Three lists with prices: the funded incumbents; the small companies that are only this; and the free-inside-a-tool alternatives.
3. **How they buy.** The purchase path, the price they already pay for anything adjacent, and any procurement or seasonal timing.
4. **How they would hear of us.** The specific channels where these customers actually are; the message that would move them; the timing (deadlines, seasons, renewal cycles); and the estimated cost to acquire one paying customer against what they would pay. A budget to break obscurity is a legitimate line here. It is spent when the brief shows it multiplies the outcome — never as a percentage of nothing.
5. **The verdict, with numbers.** An honest revenue band with a stated confidence; the single biggest risk; and the cheapest four-week test that would prove or kill the idea.

Every claim in a brief is labeled: sourced, estimated, or inferred. An inferred claim cannot carry a verdict.

### Stage 4 — The paid smoke test
Before any build: a landing page and a small spend — a few hundred dollars — in the channels the brief named, measuring whether real strangers click, sign up, or say they would pay. The kill number is written down before the first dollar goes out. This is the step that would have found the staffing-agency wallets without writing code. The owner's advertising background makes this step fast and cheap; it is used, not assumed away.

### Stage 5 — The ruling
The **portfolio seat**, not the project chat, scores the brief and the smoke test against the rubric below and rules. The seat that gathered the evidence does not write its own verdict — the same rule as builder and critic. Only the top-scoring candidate takes the pre-revenue build slot when it opens.

## The rubric

Score each line 0–3. Hard thresholds are marked; a candidate below any threshold is parked regardless of total.

| Question | Threshold |
|---|---|
| The customer does the task themselves | must be 3 |
| No free-inside-a-tool alternative | must be 2 or more |
| No small company that is only this | 2 or more |
| Buys without a salesperson | must be 3 |
| Documents or need recur on a cycle (repeat revenue) | 2 or more |
| A dated driver exists (law, deadline, season) | bonus, not required |
| Channel to reach them is named, priced, and affordable | must be 2 or more |
| Smoke test met its kill number | must pass |
| Time to first dollar under three months of build | 2 or more |

## Where it runs

- **Project chat:** runs stages 1–4 for its own product. It has the packet, the reports, the users, and the history.
- **Portfolio seat:** stage 5, blind to affection. It also holds the shelf: every ruled candidate with its score, so that when a slot opens the next project is already chosen.
- **The kit:** holds only the format and the thresholds, so every brief is comparable to every other.

## The launch plan (the second gate)

A product that passed Gate 0 still does not go public without a **launch plan**: channels, message, timing, budget, and a kill number — written in the same format as brief section 4, re-run fresh, because the landscape moves between a brief and a launch. This applies to products that passed Gate 0 a year ago and to products built before this file existed.

## Applying this to what already exists

Every built or half-built product gets a Market Brief before any further hour is spent on it. The order is set by the portfolio seat. A product that fails is parked with its brief attached, so the reason is a document, not a memory. A parked product with a finished engine is not dead: the engine is inventory, reusable as parts inside the next product that has a buyer.

## What this replaces

The July 2026 selection formula (large organizations, small definable problem, website sells it, no person-to-person contact) is folded into stage 2 and the rubric. Every build packet written before this file is treated as inventory awaiting a brief, not as an obligation.
